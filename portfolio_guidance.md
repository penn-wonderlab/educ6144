# Idea Development Portfolio

(Detailed Guidance)

## What This Assignment Is Really About

The portfolio is **your intellectual story**. It documents how you took a problem you care about, engaged with learning sciences ideas, built on the community's thinking, and arrived at a deeper understanding. There is no single "correct" format — what matters is that the portfolio convincingly shows **knowledge building in action**.

---

## Portfolio Elements: A Flexible Framework

Instead of a rigid checklist, think of your portfolio as needing to demonstrate **three qualities**. The specific elements you include are up to you, as long as the overall portfolio is substantial and coherent.

### Quality 1: Depth of Idea Development

Show that you have taken ideas seriously and deepened them over time.

**Ways to demonstrate this** (choose at least **two**):

| Element | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Developed Idea** | An idea you owned and deepened — showing initial form → community interaction → refined understanding. Could be a concept, a design principle, a critique, a framework application. |
| **Idea Lineage Map** | A visual or narrative tracing an idea's evolution across weeks, showing how readings, peers, and class discussions shaped it. |
| **Theory Application** | Taking an LS theory/framework and working it through your problem in detail — not just citing it, but *using* it to generate new insight. |
| **Conceptual Analysis** | A careful comparison, critique, or synthesis of competing theoretical perspectives relevant to your problem. |

### Quality 2: Community & Synthesis

Show that your thinking was shaped by — and contributed to — the learning community.

**Ways to demonstrate this** (choose at least **one**):

| Element | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Rise-Above Synthesis** | Integrates multiple ideas across weeks/people into a higher-level understanding. You see patterns others haven't articulated. |
| **Idea Improvement Narrative** | Document how you meaningfully improved a peer's idea (or how a peer improved yours), with evidence from Wonderbits/Perusall. |
| **Cross-Pollination Piece** | Show how an idea from one classmate's problem domain illuminated something in yours (or vice versa). |

### Quality 3: Making It Real

Take one idea from your portfolio (ideally an idea central to your interest) and develop it further to something more substantial. Do something with it outside the boundaries of this course.

**Ways to demonstrate this** (choose at least **one**):

| Element | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Stakeholder Translation** | Translate your LS-grounded thinking into a form meaningful for a non-LS audience (e.g., a policy brief, a workshop plan, a design spec, a parent guide, a blog post). |
| **Real-World Test** | Take a principle or conjecture you've developed and test it in a real context. Document what happened and what you learned. |
| **Colleague Engagement** | Present your thinking to colleagues outside this course and document their reactions, pushback, and your subsequent reflections. |
| **Put into Practice** | Take a concrete element from your actual professional context (a lesson plan, a meeting structure, a syllabus, a design process) and redesign it using the LS principles you've developed. |

### Quality 4: Literature Grounding and Topic Presentation

This is not a separate section — it's woven throughout. Your ideas should be in conversation with course readings and, where relevant, additional literature you've sought out. A strong portfolio cites **at minimum 5–8 sources** (course readings + at least 2–3 beyond the syllabus), used meaningfully — not just name-dropped.

Your in-class presentation (Weeks 9–14) is part of the portfolio. It's an opportunity to get feedback on your problem focus and emerging ideas before the final submission.

## What Does a Good Portfolio Look Like?

Below are two illustrative portfolio outlines. These are **examples, not templates** — your portfolio should reflect your own trajectory.

---

### Example A: Research-Oriented Portfolio

> **Problem Focus:** _How can knowledge building pedagogy be adapted for asynchronous online graduate courses where students never meet face-to-face?_

**Portfolio contents:**

1. **Developed Idea — "Temporal Bridging"**
   - Started as a Perusall annotation in Week 3 noting that Scardamalia's "rise above" requires synchronous energy
   - Evolved through Wonderbits discussion with 3 peers into a concept: _temporal bridging_ — design strategies that create a sense of co-presence across asynchronous contributions
   - Final version grounds the concept in Bereiter's (2002) distinction between knowledge _about_ and knowledge _of_, and Zhang et al.'s (2009) work on collective responsibility
   - Includes 3 concrete design principles with rationale

2. **Developed Idea — "Epistemic Artifacts as Community Memory"**
   - Originated from a classmate's Wonderbits post about forgetting what was discussed
   - Built into an analysis of how different artifact types (annotations, rise-aboves, concept maps) serve different knowledge-building functions
   - Draws on Bereiter & Scardamalia (2014) and connects to distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995)

3. **Rise-Above Synthesis — "Community Knowledge vs. Individual Assessment"**
   - Synthesizes a tension that surfaced across Weeks 4, 6, and 9: if knowledge building is fundamentally communal, how do we assess individual contribution without undermining the collaborative ethos?
   - Weaves together perspectives from 4 classmates' Wonderbits contributions
   - Proposes a framework distinguishing "accountable contribution" from "competitive authorship"

4. **Making It Real — Course Redesign Proposal**
   - Takes the student's actual online course (in another department) and redesigns one unit using knowledge building principles
   - Includes before/after comparison of the discussion structure
   - Grounded in DBR logic: articulates design conjectures (Sandoval, 2014) that could be tested

5. **Topic Presentation** — _Asynchronous Knowledge Building: What Gets Lost and What Might Be Gained_ (Week 11)

> **Why this works:** The portfolio has a clear through-line (the asynchronous KB problem). The two developed ideas are distinct but related. The rise-above genuinely synthesizes community thinking rather than just summarizing readings. The "making it real" artifact is practical and grounded. Literature is used as a thinking tool, not decoration.

---

### Example B: Design-Oriented Portfolio

> **Problem Focus:** _Designing AI-assisted scaffolds that support — rather than replace — students' epistemic agency in science classrooms._

**Portfolio contents:**

1. **Developed Idea — "The Agency Dial"**
   - Started as pushback on a Week 5 reading about intelligent tutoring systems
   - Developed into a conceptual framework: AI support exists on a continuum from "autopilot" (undermines agency) to "co-pilot" (supports agency) to "navigator" (student leads, AI provides terrain awareness)
   - Grounded in Damşa et al.'s (2010) epistemic agency framework and Scardamalia's (2002) knowledge building principles
   - Includes a design heuristic table mapping scaffold types to positions on the dial

2. **Idea Lineage Map** _(visual + narrative)_
   - A concept map showing how the "Agency Dial" idea evolved across 8 weeks
   - Nodes = key moments (annotations, class discussions, peer interactions, readings)
   - Edges = how each moment transformed the idea
   - Narrative annotations explain the pivotal shifts

3. **Cross-Pollination Piece — Borrowing from a Peer's Problem**
   - A classmate working on teacher professional development proposed "pedagogical routines for noticing student ideas"
   - The student adapted this into "AI routines for noticing student epistemic moves" — a concrete design pattern for the AI scaffold
   - Both students co-wrote a Wonderbits piece connecting their ideas

4. **Stakeholder Translation — Design Spec for Developers**
   - Translates the LS-grounded design thinking into a 2-page design specification that a software team could use
   - Includes user stories, design requirements, and "anti-patterns" to avoid
   - Written in accessible language with LS concepts explained parenthetically

5. **Topic Presentation** — _Can AI Support Knowledge Building Without Stealing It?_ (Week 10)

> **Why this works:** The portfolio demonstrates genuine idea development (the "Agency Dial" grows from a critical annotation into a usable framework). The idea lineage map makes the knowledge-building process visible. The cross-pollination piece shows real community engagement. The "making it real" element is creative — translating academic concepts into a different genre (design spec) — and demonstrates practical reach.

## Quality Indicators: Good vs. Great

| Dimension | Good (Meets expectations) | Great (Exceeds expectations) |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem focus** | Clearly stated and consistent | Evolves in response to learning — the portfolio shows *how and why* the focus sharpened |
| **Idea development** | Ideas are explained clearly with supporting citations | Ideas show genuine transformation over time — you can see the "before" and "after" and understand what caused the change |
| **Community engagement** | References peers' ideas | Demonstrates genuine intellectual exchange — ideas were *changed* by community interaction, not just decorated with peer citations |
| **Literature use** | Course readings are cited accurately | Literature is used as a *thinking tool* — you engage with arguments, push back, apply frameworks to generate new insight, bring in sources the course didn't assign |
| **Making it real** | An idea is applied or translated into a concrete artifact | The real-world application clearly demonstrates LS principles and generates new insights or feedback for the student |
| **Coherence** | Portfolio has identifiable sections | Portfolio reads as an integrated intellectual narrative — elements connect to each other, not just to the problem |
| **Craft** | Well-written and organized | Thoughtful choices about format, visualization, and structure that enhance communication |

## Self-Assessment Checklist

Before submitting, review your portfolio against these questions:

- [ ] Is my problem focus clear and threaded throughout?
- [ ] Can a reader trace how at least two ideas developed over the course?
- [ ] Have I shown how my thinking was genuinely shaped by the community (not just that I participated)?
- [ ] Is my literature use substantive (not just citations but real engagement with arguments)?
- [ ] Does my portfolio take at least one idea and make it "real" outside the context of this course?
- [ ] Does the portfolio feel like a coherent story, not a collection of separate assignments?
- [ ] Have I included evidence from Wonderbits/Perusall to support my claims about idea development?
- [ ] Would someone outside this class find at least one element of my portfolio interesting or useful?

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I need to include every element type listed above?**
A: No. Choose the combination that best tells your story. The minimum is: 2 demonstrations of depth + 1 of community/synthesis + 1 "making it real" element + literature woven throughout + your topic presentation. But you're welcome to do more, or to combine elements creatively (e.g., your "making it real" element could also be a developed idea).

**Q: Can elements overlap?**
A: Absolutely. A rise-above synthesis might also serve as your "making it real" artifact if it translates perspectives for an outside audience. A developed idea might include a cross-pollination piece. The categories are lenses, not boxes.

**Q: What format should the portfolio take?**
A: Use Wonderbits Document mode as one organizational tool. You can export a set of ideas to multiple formats. You can also export your own Wonderbits data via the Profile settings page. You may supplement with additional documents, slides, or other media if they strengthen the portfolio. The presentation should be uploaded as slides or a recording.

**Q: How long should the portfolio be?**
A: There's no fixed length. A strong portfolio is typically the equivalent of **8–15 pages** of written content (including visuals, maps, etc.), but quality and depth matter more than volume. The two exemplars above would each be roughly 10–12 pages.

**Q: Can I use ideas from my facilitation week?**
A: Yes! Your rise-above synthesis from facilitation could seed a portfolio element, though you'd want to develop it significantly beyond the original version.

**Q: What if my problem focus changed mid-semester?**
A: That's fine — even desirable. Document the shift and explain what prompted it. A portfolio that shows genuine intellectual evolution is stronger than one that pretends the problem was always clear.
